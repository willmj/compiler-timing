LX Scratchpad Planning

The pass decides which tensors get to live in the accelerator’s 1.55 MiB per-core scratchpad instead of round-tripping through HBM — and one of its sub-steps, LX relayout planning, measured 39% of the pass.

Spyre cores have a small, software-managed scratchpad called LX. It is not a cache: nothing evicts or fills automatically, and it is not threaded through the generated wrapper as a tensor. A buffer that lives there is handed from one kernel launch to the next by its LX offset alone. Somebody has to assign those offsets, and that somebody is _maybe_scratchpad_planning, the last of the twenty pre-scheduling passes.

Everything below about mechanism comes from the source. The timings are mine, from one small workload — scope is stated at the end.

01

What residency buys

A buffer that is produced and then read has two possible homes. In HBM, any core can address any byte, but every access pays main-memory latency. In LX, the access is core-local and fast — but each core physically holds only its own slice.

The saving is counted in reads, not accesses. The first touch is the producer’s write (or, for a graph input, an unavoidable clone-in), so the allocator’s own accounting discounts it: a buffer read only once by its producer saves nothing and is barred with no consumer reads it from LX.

02

The packing problem

Once eligibility is settled, what remains is a two-dimensional packing problem, and it is worth seeing in the allocator’s own terms. Every candidate becomes a LifetimeBoundBuffer: a name, a size, and a strictly increasing list of the operation indices that touch it. Its live range runs from the first touch to just past the last.

So each buffer is a rectangle — size tall, lifetime wide — and the solver must place all of them inside a fixed 1.55 MiB ceiling without any two overlapping in both address and time. Rectangles whose lifetimes are disjoint may share an address; that is how a slot gets reused.

The default solver, GreedyLayoutSolver, walks the transition ticks in order. At each tick it frees the buffers that just died, then places the buffers that just became live — trying in-place reuse of a dying parent first, then the space below the lowest allocation, then above the highest, then any gap in between wide enough to hold it. Anything left unplaced keeps address = None and spills.

03

Most of the work is deciding who is eligible

Here is the part that surprised me when I measured it. The packing above is cheap. The expensive part is arriving at the candidate set at all — because a buffer must clear twelve independent gates before it is even offered to the solver, and several of those gates require walking the graph.

Nothing is dropped for ineligibility: a barred buffer is still handed over carrying its residency_reason, and the solver declines to place it. The reasons survive into the log, which is how you find out why something you expected in LX is not.

The order is deliberate — cheap structural guards first, and the back-gap probe last because it touches device_layout and costs the most:

1op not allowed — the producing op is not on the 19-entry LX-reuse allowlist
2unsized — no device layout, so no computable footprint
3mutation target
4tiled (advancing) — the address moves per coarse-tile iteration, which LX cannot express
5read by restickify — a cross-frame barrier: read and write frames are transposes
6live across an extern kernel — an opaque call may launch another program that reuses the same offsets
7index tensor or indirectly accessed — data-dependent addressing must resolve from HBM
8graph output — unless boundary cloning is on
9partial or offset read
10core division mismatch — users disagree about how the buffer is split across cores
11no consumer reads it from LX — residency would save nothing
12LX back gap — supported in HBM, not in LX

Gate 6 is the one worth internalizing, because it explains why the scratchpad is so conservative. LX is shared by every compiled Spyre program. An extern kernel is opaque — its body can launch a nested compile, and that program will allocate the same LX offsets. A buffer merely live across such a call is silently overwritten, and its consumer reads another program’s data. Being live across the hazard is as fatal as being touched by it.

04

Where LX relayout planning fits

Gate 10 rejects a buffer whose users disagree about how it is split across cores. LX relayout planning exists to rescue a specific case of exactly that disagreement, and it runs before buffer generation, as the first thing _prepare_buffers does.

The hardware fact underneath it: when a buffer is resident, each core physically holds only the slice it owns. A PerCoreView records that ownership — which device dimensions are split, by how much, and which slot each core gets. If the producer writes the buffer split one way and a consumer wants to read it split another way, then a core reading its own slice of the output needs bytes from other cores’ slices of the input. LX cannot do that.

Why the search is expensive

collect_lx_relayout_plans builds a reverse index of every buffer’s readers, then for each candidate source runs a gauntlet: compute the producer’s per-core view, project ownership into loop symbols, and for every consumer check that its view is total and representable, that it uses the same core count, that it is pointwise or a two-input matmul, and that the two partitions are compatible. Any single failing consumer rejects the whole group — the copies sharing a source are all-or-nothing.

The compatibility check is the costly one. _compatible_partitions compares every source core slice against every destination core slice to confirm the mapping is a clean permutation with uniform fan-in and fan-out. That is quadratic in core count, per candidate — and core count is 32 by default. It is also the reason this cost should be expected to move with SENCORES, an axis no committed measurement has varied.

Accepted plans are not applied here. They ride into the solver attached to their source buffer, get addresses assigned as a pair, and are only materialized into the graph at the very end of _push_allocation — last, and still inside pre-scheduling, because a solver retry needs the original graph intact.

05

Measured shape

Instrumenting the ten steps of plan_allocation closes the pass to 100% (residual 0.02%). The split is lopsided in a way no static reading of the code predicted — I predicted the solver would dominate, and it is 1%:

Two things fall out. Eligibility determination — the twelve gates and the relayout search — is essentially all of the cost, and it is linear in buffer count. The packing is a rounding error but is the only superlinear term, and its exponent is climbing: 2.08×, then 2.25×, then 2.71× across successive doublings, converging toward the product of placeable buffers and transition ticks that the time-stepping loop rescans. At this scale it has four orders of magnitude to climb before it matters. It is nonetheless the term that will eventually bite.

Who actually gets in

The pass had no aggregate efficacy metric — only per-buffer debug logs — which for a pass whose whole value is residency is the one number missing. Adding a tally: at 768 operations, 512 of 768 buffers reside and 256 are barred, with 99.6% of exclusions from a single gate, splitting almost exactly in half between a K-split writer (128) and a broadcast read (127).

Neither half is an allocator-level opportunity. A K-split reduction leaves partial sums on most cores, so the data genuinely is not there to read; any remedy belongs to work division. A broadcast read is blocked on a capability the source names plainly — there is no single-base LX broadcast — so the only direction is a feature, replicating a shared buffer whole into every core’s private LX. The gate is refusing to place data that either does not exist or cannot be addressed.

One result worth following up

On this workload the relayout search costs 39% of the pass and finds nothing. Turning it off with SPYRE_LX_PLANNER_RELAYOUT=0 took the pass from 336.85 ms to 214.70 ms — a 36% reduction — with the residency gates and the solver unchanged and output identical.

That is not a bug and not yet an argument for changing anything: a workload where relayout does fire pays this search once and saves a reshuffle on every read. But it does mean the search runs unconditionally for the default greedy solver, and that its cost is paid whether or not any plan survives. Whether the trade is favorable is a question about workloads nobody has measured.

Scope, and one correction. Mechanism is from source and holds generally. The numbers are n=1 per point, no warmup discard, on a softmax-stack workload that is neither of the two families in the committed studies, against torch-spyre faff191. At 337 ms and 128 buffers this sits two orders of magnitude below the regime where the pass’s known n1.45 scaling was observed, so nothing here confirms or refutes that slope.

Driving the solver directly on synthetic buffer sets since settles its scaling: it is exactly quadratic, converging on 4.00× per doubling and tracking the buffer×tick product to N=2048. But two controls refute the obvious explanations — live-set size costs only 1.85× for 64× more overlap, and ceiling pressure is non-monotonic. So the driver is purely the nested rescan, not the gap search over the live set, and the long-lived-carry topology this workload never produced would not have changed the answer. Quadratic with a small constant: ~1.1 s extrapolated to N=4096, against real graphs that reach 128–770 buffers.
